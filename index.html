1. TECHNOLOGY (INFRASTRUCTURE)

Application of scientific knowledge, tools, and techniques to solve problems and make human activities easier, faster, or more efficient.

  • Physical tools: phones, ships, satellites
  • Systems: the internet, supply chains
  • Knowledge: software, engineering know-how
Link: Technology is what makes it possible to move information, goods, and people across distance.

2. MEDIA (CONTENT & MESSAGING)

The channels and technologies used to store, deliver, and share information, entertainment, and ideas to large audiences (McQuail, 2010).

  • Print: books, newspapers
  • Broadcast: radio, television, film
  • Digital: websites, streaming, social media
Link: Media carries ideas, culture, and news across borders.

McLuhan (1964): Media are "extensions of man," meaning they extend our senses and abilities.

3. GLOBALIZATION (INTERDEPENDENT RESULT)

The growing interconnection and interdependence of countries through the flow of goods, money, people, information, and culture across borders.

  • Giddens (1990): The intensification of worldwide social relations, so that local events are shaped by distant events.
  • Robertson (1992): The compression of the world and growing awareness of the world as a single whole.
  • Main dimensions: Economic, political, cultural, technological.
Link: Technology and media are the engines that drive globalization.
SUBTOPIC 01

DEVELOPMENT OF MEDIA & GLOBALIZATION

Definition: Media development tracks how communication tools have evolved from print to the internet, allowing ideas, culture, and messages to travel faster, farther, and cheaper. As media expands its global reach, international connections deepen—causing media and globalization to evolve together.

01. MEDIA EVOLUTION AND GLOBAL REACH

Explore how information sharing evolved from print to AI-curated video feeds.

PRINT REVOLUTION (15TH CENTURY)

Key Developments: Gutenberg's movable type (c. 1450) enabled mass reproduction of books cheap, fast, and identical.

Global Reach & Impact: Spread ideas like the Protestant Reformation and scientific discovery; 'Print Capitalism' helped people imagine shared national identity (Anderson, 1983). Limited by language translation barriers and literacy rates.

IMAGE: PRINTING PRESS & EARLY MASS PUBLISHING

TELEGRAPH & SUBSEA CABLES (1800S)

Key Developments: Electrical telegraph networks and transatlantic submarine telegraph cables decoupled communication from physical transportation speed.

Global Reach & Impact: Enabled real-time international news reporting, synchronized financial markets across continents, and coordinated global supply logistics.

IMAGE: ELECTRIC TELEGRAPH & WIRELESS COMM

BROADCAST ERA (1900S)

Key Developments: Radio networks, television, and cinema brought real-time audio and moving visual media into millions of households simultaneously.

Global Reach & Impact: Created shared global pop culture, standardized international news cycles, and fueled the rise of global brand advertising.

IMAGE: MASS BROADCAST TV & RADIO

SATELLITE AGE (1960S)

Key Developments: Geostationary communication satellites enabled instantaneous live global video broadcasts across oceans without landline limits.

Global Reach & Impact: Gave birth to Marshall McLuhan's 'Global Village'—where audiences worldwide watched breaking events live simultaneously (e.g., Moon Landing, Olympic Games).

IMAGE: GEOSTATIONARY SATELLITE NETWORKS

DIGITAL & AI REVOLUTION (1990S-PRESENT)

Key Developments: The World Wide Web, smartphones, social media networks, and algorithmic recommendation feeds democratized content creation and distribution.

Global Reach & Impact: Shifted communication from one-to-many broadcasting to interactive many-to-many networking. Driven by real-time data, algorithmic curation, and global connectivity.

Technological globalization is the worldwide spread of digital tools, communication networks, transportation systems, and technological knowledge across national borders.

GOVERNING THE GLOBAL TECHNOSCAPE

SELF-REINFORCING PROCESS GLOBAL_DISRUPT_INDEX: ACTIVE

Technological globalization is a self-reinforcing process where innovation continuously reduces distance, lowers trading costs, and connects societies more tightly than at any point in human history.

It is neither entirely positive nor inherently negative; its net impact depends entirely on how technologies are deployed and governed.

The primary goal for governments, enterprises, and individuals is not merely to increase technological connectivity, but to ensure that the resulting opportunities are equitably distributed, secure, and beneficial to all parts of society.

EMPIRICAL CASE STUDY

MIT TWITTER STUDY (2006–2017)

HUMAN BEHAVIOR > BOTS

A massive MIT study tracking Twitter data over 11 years revealed that false news spread significantly farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than the truth across all categories—especially false political news.

Key Finding: Automated bots spread true and false news at identical rates. False news spreads faster because humans are more likely to share it, driven by the psychological appeal of novel information.
